# Africa’s Blue Futures Hackathon 2027 — Motivation Statement

Scientific discovery acquires its greatest significance when it reaches beyond the laboratory and responds to the needs of society. It is this connection between scientific knowledge and practical impact that draws me to Africa’s Blue Futures Hackathon 2027.

As a Master’s student in Chemistry at the University of Dar es Salaam, my research interests lie at the intersection of computational chemistry, machine learning, data science and scientific software development. I am particularly interested in translating experimental evidence into computational models and transforming those models into tools that address environmental and industrial challenges.

My first challenge preference is microplastic contamination in farmed seaweed. For thousands of coastal households in Tanzania, seaweed is more than a marine resource; it is a livelihood, a source of economic participation and a pathway towards opportunity, particularly for women. Protecting its quality is therefore not merely an analytical challenge but an undertaking with environmental, industrial and socioeconomic significance.

In exploring this challenge, I developed SeaweedGuard, a computational platform for microplastic screening using infrared spectroscopy. Its development involved experimental data curation, spectral processing, computational modelling and external evaluation using 3,832 experimental spectra. The platform achieved 98.21% precision among accepted polymer assignments, with 63.76% acceptance coverage of polypropylene and polystyrene spectra. These findings provide a scientific foundation for translating spectroscopic measurements into actionable evidence for laboratory-supported seaweed quality monitoring.

However, identifying contaminants is only one part of the challenge. Equally important is understanding how analytical findings can inform decisions across cultivation, harvesting, drying and processing. I am interested in exploring how computational technologies, shared laboratory services and existing producer networks can bring scientific capabilities closer to the communities that need them.

During the hackathon, I would contribute computational modelling, experimental data analysis, Python programming and scientific software development, helping translate collective ideas into testable hypotheses and functional prototypes. These capabilities could also contribute to the investigation of sustainable materials for seaweed farming and the development of intelligent approaches to seafood quality assessment.

What particularly attracts me to this hackathon is the convergence of disciplines and perspectives. Chemistry offers insights into the nature of materials, marine science provides an understanding of their environmental context, and engineering and entrepreneurship create pathways towards implementation. Working across these disciplines would broaden my understanding of how scientific innovations can respond to the realities of coastal communities.

The collaboration between the University of Dar es Salaam and the University of Southern Denmark further offers an opportunity for scientific and cultural exchange around environmental challenges that transcend geographical boundaries.

Ultimately, my motivation is to contribute to a blue economy in which scientific discovery informs practical decisions, technological innovation strengthens environmental stewardship, and the benefits of marine resources extend to the communities whose livelihoods depend upon them.
